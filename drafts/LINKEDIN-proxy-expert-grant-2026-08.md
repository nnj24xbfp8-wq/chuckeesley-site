## The post

Problem validation — figuring out whether a problem is real, important, and worth solving — is the most consequential skill in entrepreneurship. After years of teaching it, I think it's also the hardest, and for a structural reason: you can't practice it without an expert on the other side of the table.

Students can read every framework and learn nothing until they're sitting across from someone who actually lives the problem, discovering thirty seconds in that the question they prepared was the wrong question. Experts are scarce. Students usually get one shot. So they spend their single real conversation learning what a rehearsal could have taught them.

I'm glad to share that the Stanford Accelerator for Learning has funded a project going straight at that constraint.

𝗣𝗿𝗼𝘅𝘆-𝗘𝘅𝗽𝗲𝗿𝘁 𝗣𝗿𝗮𝗰𝘁𝗶𝗰𝗲: 𝗔𝗻 𝗔𝗴𝗲𝗻𝘁𝗶𝗰 𝗔𝗜 𝗦𝘆𝘀𝘁𝗲𝗺 𝗳𝗼𝗿 𝗧𝗲𝗮𝗰𝗵𝗶𝗻𝗴 𝗘𝗻𝘁𝗿𝗲𝗽𝗿𝗲𝗻𝗲𝘂𝗿𝗶𝗮𝗹 𝗣𝗿𝗼𝗯𝗹𝗲𝗺 𝗩𝗮𝗹𝗶𝗱𝗮𝘁𝗶𝗼𝗻 is one of twelve projects selected under the Accelerator's AI in Teaching and Learning at Stanford: Innovation with Evidence grants. I'm running it with Itai Ashlagi and PhD students Yikai Cao and Xilan Zhang.

A proxy expert is not a tutor, and not a simulated population. It's an AI instantiation of one specific real person's problem representation — built from multi-session discovery interviews with fellows at Stanford's Distinguished Careers Institute. Students practice against the proxy through adversarial verification, where its job is to resist a sloppy problem statement the way the real person would. Then they meet the actual fellow in a live transfer study, with team-level random assignment so we can tell whether the practice actually transferred.

Most AI-in-education work casts the model as a tutor that explains, or a simulated population that stands in for respondents. This is a third thing: 𝗔𝗜 𝗮𝘀 𝗲𝗺𝗯𝗼𝗱𝗶𝗲𝗱 𝗽𝗿𝗼𝗯𝗹𝗲𝗺. Not there to teach you, not there to represent a market — there to be a hard, specific, opinionated instance of the thing you're trying to understand.

If it holds up, it should generalize to any project-based course where the scarce resource is access to someone who genuinely knows. Which is most of them. Fourteen months, run through my Stanford courses, and I'll share what we find including the parts that don't work.

Next stop: Shenzhen, August 20 – September 10, as Faculty Director of the Stanford BOSP Shenzhen Global Seminar. Fifteen Stanford undergraduates spending three weeks on how the Shenzhen hardware ecosystem actually works, building hardware startups alongside InnoX.

Both projects are attempts to close the same gap — between learning about something and actually encountering it. Whether that's an expert's view of a problem, or a supply chain you only understand by walking it.
