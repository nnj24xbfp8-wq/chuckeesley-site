## The post

Some skills can only be learned by practicing them against an expert. Entrepreneurial problem validation is one. So are clinical diagnosis, legal client intake, and diplomatic negotiation. Your competence only becomes visible when someone who actually knows the domain refuses to accept a shallow framing of the problem.

Teaching them is not the hard part. A student can read The Mom Test, work through Steve Blank's customer discovery material, complete every case in the syllabus, and still fall apart in the first ten minutes with a live problem-holder.

What is missing is practice, and practice gets rationed twice. Expert time is scarce and unevenly distributed: students inside well-resourced institutions and dense professional networks get plenty of it, and everyone else gets very little. Then, even when an expert is in the room, the social situation suppresses the exact behaviors a novice needs to rehearse. Asking the naive question. Disagreeing out loud with someone more senior. Being visibly wrong four times in a row.

The Stanford Accelerator for Learning has funded a project going at that bottleneck, one of twelve selected under its AI in Teaching and Learning at Stanford: Innovation with Evidence grants. I am running it with Itai Ashlagi and PhD students Yikai Cao and Xilan Zhang.

A proxy expert is not a tutor and not a simulated population. It is an AI instantiation of one specific, consenting, named person's representation of a problem, built from multi-session discovery interviews. Our source experts are fellows at Stanford's Distinguished Careers Institute, taking part as research partners rather than as subjects. Students engage the proxy the way they would engage the fellow, fail against it repeatedly at no social cost, and then meet the actual person.

The proxy was never the destination. It is rehearsal, and the point of rehearsal is to make scarce human contact worth more when it finally happens.

Educational AI has mostly settled into three roles: the model tutors, assists with writing, or simulates a population. This is a fourth. AI as an embodied problem, held at arm's length for the learner to grapple with, grounded in one named person's articulated view rather than a theory or an average.

The question I care most about is also the one the field has answered least: do gains from AI-mediated practice transfer to real expert engagement? Our primary outcome is the quality of the human conversation that follows. If the proxy does not improve the real encounters, we will report that.

Next: I am in Shenzhen from August 20 to September 10 as Faculty Director of the Stanford BOSP Shenzhen Global Seminar, where fifteen Stanford undergraduates will spend three weeks on how the Shenzhen hardware ecosystem actually works, building hardware startups alongside InnoX.

This will be my fifth trip to Shenzhen, after 2017, 2019, 2023, and a short visit this past June. Every one of those was a matter of days, so three weeks will be the first stretch long enough to work in the place rather than look at it. I have been reading Juan Du's The Shenzhen Experiment to prepare, which is very good on the institutional history and a useful corrective to the fishing-village-to-metropolis story everyone repeats.
