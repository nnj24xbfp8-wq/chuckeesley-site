Problem validation — working out whether a problem is real, important, and worth solving — is the most consequential skill in entrepreneurship. It is also the one I have never found a good way to teach.

The reason is structural. You cannot practice it without an expert on the other side of the table. A student can read every framework and still learn nothing until they sit across from someone who actually lives the problem and discovers, thirty seconds in, that the question they prepared is the wrong question. Experts are scarce, their time is expensive, and a student typically gets one shot. So the skill that matters most gets the least practice, and students burn their single real conversation learning things they could have learned in a rehearsal.

The Stanford Accelerator for Learning has funded a project to attack exactly that constraint. **Proxy-Expert Practice: An Agentic AI System for Teaching Entrepreneurial Problem Validation** is one of twelve projects selected under the [AI in Teaching and Learning at Stanford: Innovation with Evidence](https://acceleratelearning.stanford.edu/funding/ai-in-teaching-and-learning-at-stanford-innovation-and-evidence-grants/) grants. I am running it with **Itai Ashlagi** and PhD students **Yikai Cao** and **Xilan Zhang**.

### What a proxy expert is

Not a tutor, and not a simulated population. A *proxy expert* is an AI instantiation of one specific real person's problem representation — how a particular expert understands their own domain, what they consider urgent, and what they would push back on.

We build each one from multi-session discovery interviews with fellows at Stanford's **Distinguished Careers Institute**. DCI fellows are accomplished people in the middle of a deliberate career transition, which makes them unusually good subjects: they have decades of domain depth and they are actively rethinking the problems in their field rather than defending settled positions.

Students then practice against the proxy through adversarial verification — the proxy's job is to resist a sloppy problem statement the way the real person would. Only after that do they meet the actual fellow, in a live transfer study. That last step is the part that makes this research rather than a product demo: we find out whether practice against the proxy improves performance with the human, using team-level random assignment for causal identification.

### Why the framing matters

Most AI-in-education work casts the model as a **tutor** that explains, or as a **simulated population** that stands in for survey respondents. This is a third thing: AI as *embodied problem*. The system is not there to teach you or to represent a market. It is there to be a hard, specific, opinionated instance of the thing you are trying to understand.

If that framing holds up, it generalizes past entrepreneurship to any experiential, project-based education where the scarce resource is access to someone who genuinely knows. Which is most of them.

The project runs 14 months, through my courses at Stanford. I will write up what we find, including the parts that do not work — and on a project premised on students discovering their questions were wrong, it would be poor form to assume ours are right.
